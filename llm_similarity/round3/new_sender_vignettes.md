# Draft vignette set — attention × beliefs representations

## Design

This draft contains 48 sender-side vignettes: eight for each combination of attention category
(Moral, Self-interest, Mutual Benefit/Cooperation) and belief (High, Low). Within every
representation, vignettes 1–4 concern interactions among individuals and vignettes 5–8 concern
firms, entrepreneurs, managers, or other market interactions.

The belief dimension is defined as the protagonist's expectation about the counterpart's response,
not the counterpart's realized behavior:

- **High belief:** the protagonist expects the counterpart to respond favorably to the protagonist,
  for example by accepting proposed terms, repaying an advance, performing a promised action, or
  sharing proceeds.
- **Low belief:** the protagonist expects the counterpart not to respond favorably, for example by
  rejecting proposed terms, failing to repay or perform, or retaining the proceeds.

The expectation is stated before the protagonist chooses. What primarily frames the protagonist's
decision remains the attention category:

- **Moral:** what is right, deserved, needed, or appropriately divided.
- **Self-interest:** the protagonist's own material payoff, use, or protection.
- **Mutual Benefit/Cooperation:** the additional value the two sides could create together.

The vignettes deliberately vary the resource, relationship, setting, decision structure, and form
of the counterpart's possible response. The words banned in the previous generation protocol are
not used in the vignette texts.

## Moral — High belief

**MH1 — Individuals; family succession.** Elena receives the keys to a lakeside cabin left to her alone, although her cousin spent years maintaining it without pay. She can sell the cabin herself or place it in both names before the sale. Her cousin has always documented expenses carefully and Elena expects him to divide the proceeds exactly as they agree. Even so, her decision turns on what his years of work deserve. She reviews the deed and decides how much ownership to assign him.

**MH2 — Individuals; emergency loan.** Malik has nine hundred dollars from a tax refund when a close friend asks for help replacing a broken furnace during a cold spell. The friend has repaid every previous loan on schedule, and Malik is confident this one would also come back. Malik nevertheless treats repayment as secondary: the household has no heat, while his refund was meant for a holiday. He considers what the difference in their immediate needs calls for and chooses how much to lend.

**MH3 — Individuals; community equipment.** Ruth owns a portable generator during a neighborhood blackout. An elderly neighbor needs electricity for refrigerated medicine and offers to return the machine with a full fuel tank. Ruth has seen how carefully the neighbor handles borrowed equipment and expects the promise to be honored. Several comforts in Ruth's own home would go without power while it is away. She decides how many hours the neighbor's medical need warrants giving up the generator.

**MH4 — Individuals; one-off refund.** Jun and another traveler are mistakenly issued a single eight-hundred-euro compensation voucher after both lose luggage on the same flight. The airline places the voucher in Jun's account and leaves any division to him. The other traveler offers to redeem it and transfer Jun's agreed portion afterward; her organized records make him expect she will do so. Jun first decides what portion each traveler should receive, given that her losses were larger than his.

**MH5 — Market; completion bonus.** Aditya manages a construction contract and receives a large completion bonus that the contract assigns entirely to his firm. A specialist subcontractor solved the delay that made the early finish possible but has no claim to the bonus. The subcontractor has honored every warranty commitment, so Aditya expects continued support after payment. Aditya focuses on how much of the reward the subcontractor's contribution merits and decides what additional payment to authorize.

**MH6 — Market; scarce inventory.** Beatriz owns a wholesale bakery when a shortage leaves her with one final pallet of flour. A small retailer and her own storefront could both use it, but the retailer faces closure without stock. The retailer has paid every invoice promptly, and Beatriz expects payment on the usual date. Her decision is not mainly about collection risk; she weighs the retailer's more urgent position against her own claim to the goods and chooses how much to release.

**MH7 — Market; acquisition proceeds.** Calvin sells his software company and receives an earn-out issued solely in his name. An independent consultant wrote much of the original code under a contract that promised no ownership. The consultant has agreed to support the buyer through the transition, and Calvin expects him to complete every handover task. Before signing the payment instructions, Calvin considers what share of the earn-out properly recognizes the consultant's contribution and decides how much to transfer.

**MH8 — Market; insurance recovery.** Dalia runs an orchard and receives an insurance payment after a storm destroys packed fruit awaiting collection. A local distributor had already paid transport deposits and spent days arranging replacement buyers, although the policy names only Dalia's orchard. The distributor has a long record of honoring settlements, so Dalia expects any future adjustment to be handled as agreed. She decides how much of the payment should go to the distributor in light of the losses each side bore.

## Moral — Low belief

**ML1 — Individuals; inherited savings.** Farah receives a savings account from an aunt who had often said it was meant to help both Farah and her brother. Legally the account belongs only to Farah. Her brother has broken several repayment promises, and she expects that any money handed over will never return. Still, Farah thinks the aunt's expressed intention and her brother's current need deserve weight apart from his record. She decides how much of the account to give him outright.

**ML2 — Individuals; shared repair.** Gideon receives a municipal reimbursement covering the full cost of repairs to a wall shared with his neighbor, although the neighbor paid half the original bill. The payment arrives only in Gideon's name. His neighbor has ignored earlier requests to settle small shared expenses, so Gideon doubts she would return any overpayment. He nonetheless considers which portion of the reimbursement belongs to each of them and decides how much to pass across the property line.

**ML3 — Individuals; anonymous fundraiser.** Hyejin controls the proceeds of an online fundraiser created for two families displaced by the same fire. One recipient can verify expenses; the other has repeatedly withheld receipts, and Hyejin expects no accounting if funds are sent. The platform gives her sole discretion over the division. Rather than treating poor documentation as automatically decisive, she weighs the families' losses and what each should receive, then chooses how much to release to the less reliable recipient.

**ML4 — Individuals; rescue supplies.** Isaac reaches a remote shelter with the only box of emergency food after a hiking group becomes stranded. Another hiker asks to carry half to a second shelter and promises to return any unused packets. Isaac has watched him disregard previous group plans and expects nothing to come back. Yet the people at the second shelter may have greater need. Isaac decides how much food their circumstances justify placing in the other hiker's pack.

**ML5 — Market; sales commission.** Jocelyn manages a property agency and receives a commission on a building sale that formally credits her alone. A departing salesperson found the buyer and conducted most viewings but missed the final meeting. The salesperson has already withheld client files and Jocelyn expects no help with the closing once paid. She nevertheless asks what portion of the commission the earlier work warrants and decides how much the agency should transfer.

**ML6 — Market; rent concession.** Karim owns a small commercial unit whose tenant loses stock during a flood and asks for a two-month rent reduction. The tenant has previously missed deadlines and Karim expects further delays even if he grants relief. The lease gives him the right to demand the full amount. Karim separates that concern from the tenant's present losses, considers what burden each side should bear after the disaster, and decides how much rent to waive.

**ML7 — Market; supplier settlement.** Leona's restaurant receives a refund after contaminated oil ruins an entire weekend of service. A freelance caterer who shared the delivery also lost prepared food, but the supplier sends the full settlement to Leona. The caterer has disputed past accounts, and Leona expects him to challenge any calculation she proposes. She still considers how the documented losses should be divided and chooses what portion of the settlement to offer him.

**ML8 — Market; royalty allocation.** Mateo licenses a packaging design and receives all royalties because the registration is in his name. A former business partner developed several essential prototypes but left before filing. Mateo expects the former partner to refuse the release needed for future revisions even after receiving money. He nonetheless views the prototypes as deserving recognition independent of that expected response. He decides what share of the current royalties to send.

## Self-interest — High belief

**SH1 — Individuals; interest-bearing loan.** Noura has two thousand euros sitting in a low-yield account. A friend offers to borrow it for three months and repay it with substantially more interest than the bank provides. The friend has repaid several larger loans early, so Noura expects the promised amount to arrive. She compares the return with the inconvenience of losing access to the cash and decides how much to lend based on which option leaves her financially better off.

**SH2 — Individuals; equipment exchange.** Omar owns a rarely used pressure washer. His neighbor offers to service Omar's lawn mower in exchange for borrowing it over the weekend. The neighbor is a meticulous mechanic who has completed every previous repair, and Omar expects the mower to come back working better than before. Omar is not concerned with the neighbor's project; he calculates the repair cost he can avoid and the wear on the washer, then decides whether the exchange benefits him.

**SH3 — Individuals; ticket resale.** Paloma holds a premium concert ticket she can no longer use. A stranger offers a high price through an escrow service that has already verified the funds. Paloma expects payment to clear as soon as the ticket transfers. She pays little attention to how much the stranger wants to attend; her question is whether the offer exceeds what she might obtain by waiting. She checks recent prices and chooses whether to sell now.

**SH4 — Individuals; paid online task.** Quentin owns a specialized graphics tablet that an online acquaintance wants to borrow remotely through a secure access system. In return, the acquaintance will finish a paid editing job assigned to Quentin and has completed similar jobs reliably before. Quentin expects the finished files on time. He weighs only the fee he can collect against the small risk of device damage and decides how many hours of access to grant.

**SH5 — Market; trade credit.** Rina operates a parts wholesaler. A repair shop asks to take a large order on thirty-day credit at a price that gives Rina an unusually strong margin. The shop has paid every invoice early and recently secured a major fleet contract, so she expects full payment. Rina's concern is the return on inventory compared with other available buyers. She calculates the margin and decides how much stock to release on credit.

**SH6 — Market; distribution contract.** Stefan has developed a kitchen appliance and can license a distributor to sell it abroad. The distributor offers Stefan a high royalty and provides audited records of punctual payments to other inventors. Stefan expects accurate sales reports and complete remittances. He is interested in the income the agreement can produce for him, not in expanding the distributor's business. He compares the royalty with selling domestically and decides how much territory to license.

**SH7 — Market; retention payment.** Talia manages a logistics company facing an expensive delivery deadline. An independent driver offers to take the route if paid a substantial completion premium. The driver has never missed an assigned delivery, and Talia expects the cargo to arrive on time. She calculates the penalty her company avoids, subtracts the premium, and decides how much extra compensation still leaves the company ahead. The driver's own earnings do not enter her calculation beyond securing performance.

**SH8 — Market; equipment lease.** Ugo owns an idle commercial printer. A neighboring firm offers a monthly lease payment well above Ugo's storage savings and provides a bank guarantee covering damage and late payment. Ugo expects the equipment and each payment to arrive as specified. He evaluates the proposal by the income it adds to his firm relative to keeping the printer available for possible orders, then chooses the minimum lease price he will accept.

## Self-interest — Low belief

**SL1 — Individuals; cash advance.** Valerie keeps money aside for a professional certification. A relative asks to borrow most of it and promises repayment before the enrollment deadline. The relative has repeatedly postponed earlier repayments, so Valerie expects the money to remain unpaid when she needs it. She focuses on preserving her own career plans rather than on what the relative could buy. She opens her banking app and decides whether any amount can leave the account.

**SL2 — Individuals; borrowed vehicle.** Waleed owns a van needed for his weekend delivery work. A neighbor asks to borrow it on Saturday and says it will be returned with fuel before the first delivery. The neighbor has returned vehicles late and damaged before, so Waleed expects to lose at least one paid route. He considers the earnings and repair costs he could sacrifice, not the neighbor's reason for traveling, and decides whether to hand over the keys.

**SL3 — Individuals; one-off payment.** Ximena sells a collectible watch to a stranger who proposes paying only after receiving and inspecting it. The stranger refuses escrow and has changed contact details twice, leading Ximena to expect that payment will disappear once the watch is released. Her attention is on retaining the watch or securing its price. She declines to treat the stranger's promised resale opportunity as relevant and decides what payment conditions protect her proceeds.

**SL4 — Individuals; anonymous file exchange.** Yusuf owns a set of digital templates that produces steady licensing income. An anonymous buyer asks for the editable source files, promising a larger payment after checking them. Similar buyers have copied his work without paying, and this account has no history, so Yusuf expects the promised transfer not to arrive. He weighs the possible payment against the loss of future sales and decides how much access, if any, to provide.

**SL5 — Market; supplier deposit.** Zora runs a catering company and is asked to pay a large deposit to a new produce supplier offering unusually low prices. The supplier has missed two sample deliveries and refuses a performance bond, so Zora expects the main order to arrive late or incomplete. Her decision centers on avoiding losses to her company. She compares the apparent discount with replacement costs and decides how much, if anything, to commit in advance.

**SL6 — Market; consignment sale.** Anders manufactures custom bicycles. A retailer asks to take several models on consignment and remit payment after they sell. Recent vendors report months of unpaid balances, so Anders expects the retailer to retain the revenue. He is concerned with recovering his production costs and preserving scarce inventory, not with helping the retailer attract customers. He decides how many bicycles, if any, can leave his warehouse under those terms.

**SL7 — Market; performance contract.** Bruna manages a hotel that needs urgent electrical work. A contractor requests most of the fee before starting, despite having abandoned another nearby job after receiving an advance. Bruna expects the work to remain unfinished if payment goes first. She calculates the hotel's exposure from closed rooms and a second contractor's fee, then chooses a payment schedule designed to protect the hotel's cash and operations.

**SL8 — Market; revenue platform.** Chen owns a small publishing firm. A new digital platform promises high sales but keeps all customer payments for ninety days before remitting the publisher's share. Its founders have dissolved two earlier platforms with balances outstanding, so Chen expects delayed revenue never to reach him. He considers only the firm's royalties, cash flow, and control over its catalogue, then decides how many titles to list.

## Mutual Benefit/Cooperation — High belief

**CH1 — Individuals; shared harvest.** Deepa has a greenhouse full of seedlings but no sunny ground. Her neighbor has an unused garden plot and offers to water and stake the plants if Deepa supplies them, with the harvest divided between them. The neighbor has tended shared plots successfully for years, so Deepa expects the work to be done. Together they could grow far more produce than either could manage separately. Deepa decides how many trays to place in the neighbor's care.

**CH2 — Individuals; joint renovation.** Emil owns carpentry tools but lacks money for materials. A friend has cash and a neglected workspace that could be renovated and rented, proposing to fund the supplies while Emil does the work and they divide the rent. The friend has completed two similar arrangements exactly as promised, so Emil expects the proceeds to be shared. The combined project creates an income neither can obtain alone. Emil decides how many weeks of labor to commit.

**CH3 — Individuals; one-off transport.** Fatima and another traveler meet beside two stranded motorcycles. Fatima carries repair tools but little spare fuel; the other traveler has fuel but cannot fix either engine. He follows technical instructions carefully, giving Fatima confidence that he will provide the fuel once both engines run. Pooling what they have gets both travelers home, while acting separately leaves both stuck. Fatima decides how much time and equipment to put into the repairs.

**CH4 — Individuals; online production.** Gabriel composes music but lacks animation skills. An online artist proposes that Gabriel license several tracks while she produces videos, with advertising revenue divided automatically by the hosting platform. Her previous collaborations were completed on schedule, so Gabriel expects every video to be delivered. Neither person's work earns much alone, but the combined channel could attract a larger audience. Gabriel chooses how many unreleased tracks to contribute.

**CH5 — Market; combined storefront.** Helena owns a bookshop beside a café run by another proprietor. Opening an interior passage and coordinating events could bring café customers to the books and readers to the café. The proprietor has met every shared advertising commitment, so Helena expects the renovation payment and event staffing as promised. Their projections show higher revenue for both businesses than either can generate separately. Helena decides how much capital to commit.

**CH6 — Market; pooled purchasing.** Idris manages a furniture workshop whose timber orders are too small for wholesale pricing. Another workshop proposes pooling orders to reach the supplier's minimum and arranging delivery through its warehouse. Audited invoices from previous joint purchases make Idris expect his full allotment of timber. The lower input price would let both workshops accept larger contracts. Idris chooses how much cash and storage capacity to place into the combined order.

**CH7 — Market; technology licensing.** Johanna's startup owns a battery design but lacks manufacturing capacity. A factory offers production lines and quality control in exchange for access to the design and a share of sales. The factory has fulfilled every milestone in earlier licensing projects, so Johanna expects units and revenue reports on schedule. Joining their assets could create a product neither firm can sell independently. Johanna decides how broad a license to grant.

**CH8 — Market; shared distribution.** Kwame manages a regional food producer with half-empty delivery trucks. A beverage company serving the same towns proposes filling the unused space and sharing fuel and depot costs. Its route records are precise, leading Kwame to expect timely loading and payment. Combining shipments lowers costs for both firms and opens smaller towns that neither can serve profitably alone. Kwame decides how much truck capacity to reserve for the arrangement.

## Mutual Benefit/Cooperation — Low belief

**CL1 — Individuals; community workshop.** Lidia owns woodworking equipment, while a neighbor has access to reclaimed timber. They could build market stalls for both households at far lower cost by combining the equipment and timber. The neighbor has repeatedly abandoned shared projects, so Lidia expects to be left with most of the labor. The potential saving still exists only if both contribute. She decides how many tools and weekends to commit despite doubts that the neighbor will finish his part.

**CL2 — Individuals; shared childcare.** Musa and a friend work alternating shifts and could exchange childcare hours, allowing both to accept more paid work without hiring sitters. The friend has cancelled several arrangements at the last minute, so Musa expects some promised evenings not to be covered. A reliable exchange would leave both with more income and time than arranging care separately. Musa reviews his schedule and decides how many of his own evenings to offer first.

**CL3 — Individuals; emergency crossing.** Noemi reaches a flooded crossing with a rope but cannot anchor it alone. Another stranded hiker has the climbing hardware and proposes securing the far end after Noemi crosses first. His careless handling of equipment makes her expect that he may not complete the anchor. If both perform their roles, each can cross and keep valuable supplies dry; neither can do so alone. Noemi decides whether to hand over part of her rope and step into the water.

**CL4 — Individuals; anonymous resale.** Pavel owns rare replacement parts, while an online acquaintance has access to buyers abroad. They could bundle the parts and sell them for much more than Pavel can earn locally, then divide the revenue. The acquaintance refuses escrow and has stopped answering during earlier deals, so Pavel expects incomplete payment. The larger joint return requires sending the parts first. Pavel chooses how many, if any, to put into the shipment.

**CL5 — Market; joint bid.** Rasha manages an engineering consultancy that can design a public project but lacks the field crews required for the contract. A construction firm proposes a joint bid, contributing crews while Rasha supplies the design. The firm has withdrawn from two bids after partners invested in preparation, so Rasha expects it may do so again. Winning would create substantial work for both companies that neither can secure alone. Rasha decides how much staff time to invest in the proposal.

**CL6 — Market; shared warehouse.** Soren owns an import business with excess warehouse space. A retailer proposes sharing the facility and funding a new loading system that would reduce handling costs for both firms. The retailer is behind on several commercial leases, so Soren expects its payments to lapse. The equipment becomes worthwhile only with both volumes passing through the warehouse. Soren decides how much space and capital to commit to the arrangement.

**CL7 — Market; product launch.** Tamara's company has developed a medical sensor but needs a distributor's sales network to launch it. A distributor offers market access in exchange for exclusive rights and promises a large promotional campaign. Its recent launches received little advertised support, so Tamara expects weak follow-through. A properly executed partnership could generate sales neither side can achieve separately. Tamara decides how much exclusivity and inventory to place at the distributor's disposal.

**CL8 — Market; waste exchange.** Viktor manages a brewery whose spent grain is costly to discard. A livestock producer could use it as feed and offers regular collection, which would lower disposal costs for the brewery and input costs for the producer. The producer has missed many scheduled pickups, so Viktor expects grain to accumulate unexpectedly. The gains require dependable handling by both firms. Viktor decides how much storage and production planning to reorganize around the proposed exchange.
